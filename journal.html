October 26, 2025

Continuous verbs in context

Students can form continuous verbs perfectly in grammar exercises. Then they use simple present when continuous is needed. "I watch TV" instead of "I am watching TV right now." They understand the conjugation but miss the usage context.

The issue is not formation. It is recognition of when continuous applies. Students are not spotting time indicators like "now," "at the moment," "this week," or "currently." Without those cues, they default to simple present even when describing ongoing actions.

Product idea: timeline activity focused on time indicators

Build a visual timeline game where students identify time indicators first, then place actions on the timeline. Highlight words like "now," "this week," "currently" before asking about verb forms. The timeline reveals the context that determines continuous vs simple present.

Next steps: Create scenarios with mixed time indicators. Let students label which indicators signal continuous tense. Then ask them to choose the correct verb form based on placement on the timeline.

Comparatives in real context

Week 3: students are still mixing up "more interesting" and "more boring." I am also seeing "interestinger" in their writing. It is wrong, but it tracks with how patterns form in English.

I scrapped the flashcards midway through Tuesday. Comparatives are relational. You need a reference point to make meaning. Memorizing "fast → faster" without "faster than what?" is empty practice. The goal is not vocabulary lists. The goal is a pattern with many exceptions, and that requires context.

I had tried to push "more/less than" and "whereas," but the foundation was not solid. We reset and reviewed core comparatives from the start. I grouped connectors like "because" and "while" with the review. That pairing seemed to help students explain their choices, not only label them.

Product idea: drag-and-drop with real comparisons

Show a race car and a bicycle. Prompt: "The car is _____ than the bike." Students drag the correct word into place. Meaning comes from the comparison, not from recalling a single adjective. The same could scale to "bigger," "quieter," "more helpful," and so on.

Layer in audio: pair visuals with short sounds. "The elephant is louder than the mouse." Let students hear the contrast while they place the words. Audio plus visuals plus interaction should outperform paper cards.

Next steps: Build a small drag-and-drop prototype with image pairs and three distractors. Add a minimal "because/while" prompt after each answer to reinforce reasoning. Track which adjective pairs cause the most errors and surface them more often.

Iterating on ESOL games

Built these games because flashcards were not working for students. Version 1 lived on a Blogger site. It was functional but rough. Version 2 cleaned up the code and made interactions smoother. Version 3 added challenge and practice modes.

Each rewrite taught me something. Version 1: Ship something that solves the problem. Do not wait for perfect code. Version 2: Make it maintainable or future maintenance becomes painful. Version 3: Small UX changes can transform how useful a tool feels.

Practice mode shows correct answers immediately. Challenge mode times responses and tracks streaks. Same content, different learning styles. Some students prefer practice mode for low-pressure repetition. Others thrive on the timer in challenge mode. Offering both modes serves different needs.

Next steps: Track which mode students use most often. Surface streaks and time data to show progress. Consider adding a custom mode where students set their own goals.

August 8, 2025

Building for myself first

Built the ADHD First Aid Kit because standard productivity apps do not account for how my brain works. They assume linear thinking and easy focus. That is not my experience.

I kept hitting walls where processing would just stop. Not procrastination or decision paralysis, but actual cognitive barriers. I needed tools that match how I function. I made a toolkit with timers, focus tools, and clean interfaces that do not add sensory load. All in one place, designed for non-linear thinking.

Product principle: solve authentically

When I showed this to other neurodivergent people, they asked to use it. That is how I know it is working. If I solve my problem honestly, I likely solve theirs too. Build for real needs, not for assumed needs.

Next steps: Add body doubling scheduling to the toolkit. Create more visual options for habit tracking. Keep testing with ADHD users to find what actually helps.
