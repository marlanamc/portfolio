October 26, 2025

Continuous verbs in context

Watching students in class today, I noticed something important. They can form continuous verbs perfectly in structured exercises, but then struggle in conversation. Someone will say "I watch TV" when they mean "I am watching TV right now." They know the conjugation — that's not the issue. They just can't see when to use it.

I realize students aren't noticing the time indicators that signal continuous tense. Words like "now," "at the moment," "this week," or "currently" pass by unnoticed. Without those cues, students default to simple present because it feels safer, even when describing ongoing actions. This isn't a memory problem. It's a pattern recognition problem.

I'm thinking about a timeline activity that makes those indicators visible. What if students identify time markers first, before choosing verb forms? Show a visual timeline, highlight words like "now" and "currently," then ask students to place actions. The timeline would make the relationship between time markers and verb choice explicit.

My next step: Create mixed scenarios where students actively label which indicators signal continuous tense. Then they choose verb forms based on placement on the timeline. I want to slow down the recognition process so students can see the patterns before they need to use them in conversation.

Comparatives in real context

Watching students tackle comparatives reminds me why teaching is both challenging and rewarding. They can memorize "fast → faster" but miss the relational nature of the concept. A comparative needs context: faster than what? Without that reference point, the language feels hollow.

I tried rushing into advanced structures like "more/less than" and "whereas," but realized I was pushing too fast. We needed to slow down and build the foundation properly. I went back to core comparatives and paired them with connectors like "because" and "while." Something clicked — students started explaining their choices instead of just labeling words. They were thinking through the logic, not just matching patterns.

I'm designing a drag-and-drop activity that makes comparisons tangible. Show a race car and a bicycle side by side. Ask: "The car is _____ than the bike." Students drag words into place, but the meaning comes from seeing both objects together. The same principle scales: bigger, quieter, more helpful — any comparative needs two points of reference.

Adding audio layer: Students hear "The elephant is louder than the mouse" with the actual sounds. They feel the contrast while placing words. Three modes of input — audio, visual, tactile — working together. This has to be better than flipping through flashcards.

Next steps: Build a prototype with image pairs and three distractors. Add a simple "because/while" prompt after each answer to reinforce reasoning. Track which adjective pairs cause the most confusion and surface them more frequently. I want students to understand comparatives deeply, not just recite them.

Iterating on ESOL games

I built these games because I watched students lose engagement with flashcards. Some would get frustrated, others would check out entirely. They needed something different. Version 1 went live on a Blogger site — rough around the edges but it worked. Version 2 smoothed out the code and interactions. Version 3 introduced challenge and practice modes to meet different learning needs.

Each iteration taught me something crucial. Version 1: Ship something that helps students now, even if the code isn't perfect. Version 2: Invest in maintainability or you'll struggle later. Version 3: Small UX changes can completely change how students experience learning.

Now I see students choosing what works for them. Practice mode shows answers immediately — perfect for building confidence. Challenge mode adds a timer and tracks streaks — students who want to push themselves love this. Same content, tailored experiences. Seeing students engage because they have options makes me keep iterating.

Next steps: Track which mode helps which students. Show progress data — streaks, times, improvements. I'm considering a custom mode where students set their own goals. I want to give students agency in how they learn, not just what they learn.

Building for myself first

I built the ADHD First Aid Kit because I couldn't find tools that understood how my brain works. Every productivity app tells me to "just focus" or "break tasks into smaller steps," but they assume linear thinking and easy concentration. That's not how my brain functions, and I was tired of feeling like I was failing systems instead of the systems failing me.

I kept hitting these moments where my processing would just stop. Not procrastination, not decision paralysis — actual cognitive walls where information just wouldn't compute. I needed tools that matched how I actually function. So I built a toolkit: timers that don't judge, focus tools that respect my limits, clean interfaces that don't overwhelm. Everything in one place, designed for non-linear thinking.

Product principle: solve authentically

When I showed this to other neurodivergent people, they immediately asked to use it. That's how I knew it was working. If I solve my own problem honestly, I likely solve theirs too. I'm building for real needs I've experienced, not for what other people assume I need.

Next steps: Add body doubling scheduling because accountability helps. Create more visual options for habit tracking since calendars don't work for me. Keep testing with ADHD users — I want to know what actually helps, not what should theoretically help.
