October 26, 2025

Continuous verbs in context

Okay so my students can write "I am watching" perfectly in a grammar worksheet. Then yesterday Maria says "I watch TV" when telling me about her evening, meaning RIGHT NOW. It clicked: they know how to form it but not when to use it.

The pattern I'm seeing: students memorize the conjugation but miss the context cues. They'll say "I watch TV" when they mean "I'm watching TV right now" because they don't recognize time indicators like "now" or "at the moment."

Got an idea brewing for a game that focuses purely on spotting those indicators. Show a timeline, highlight words like "now," "this week," "currently," and let students place actions on it. Visual timeline with scenarios instead of endless drills. Might actually work.

Comparative adjectives are harder than they look

Week 3 of comparatives and they're still mixing up "more interesting" vs "more boring." Also "interestinger" keeps showing up in their writing, which honestly makes perfect sense to me even though it's wrong.

Threw out the flashcards halfway through Tuesday's class. They're relational - you need context to know what you're comparing. Can't memorize "fast → faster" without knowing fast compared to WHAT. Students aren't just learning vocabulary here, they're learning a pattern with enough exceptions to be frustrating.

Spent too much time trying to teach "more/less than..." and "whereas" when they didn't have the basics down. Had to go back and review comparatives from scratch. Also taught connectors like "because" and "while" as a group - seemed to click better that way.

Had an idea for a drag-and-drop game: show a race car vs a bicycle, ask "The car is _____ than the bike." Visual comparison instead of rote memorization. The comparison creates the meaning, not just recalling a word.

Could add audio too. They hear "The elephant is louder than the mouse" with actual sound. Audio + visuals + dragging the words into place. Way better than me holding up index cards.

Why I rewrote my ESOL games three times

Built these games because flashcards weren't working. First version lived on my Blogger site - quick and dirty but it worked. Second version this summer: finally cleaned up the janky code and made it actually interactive. Third version today: added challenge vs practice modes.

Each rewrite taught me something different. Version 1: Ship something that solves the problem, don't wait for perfect code. Version 2: Make it maintainable or you'll hate yourself later. Version 3: Tiny UX changes can completely change how useful a tool is.

Practice mode shows answers right away. Challenge mode times them and tracks streaks. Same content, totally different vibe. Carlos only uses practice mode. Maria loves the timer in challenge mode. Offering both was the right call.

Building for myself first

Built the ADHD First Aid Kit because I was stuck and needed something that actually worked. Every productivity app tells me to "just focus" or "break tasks into smaller steps." Cool, my brain doesn't do linear thinking though.

Kept hitting these walls where my brain just... wouldn't process information. Not decision paralysis, just actual processing failures. So I made a toolkit of things that actually help me: timers that don't judge, focus tools, clean interfaces that don't overwhelm. Put it all in one place.

Showed it to some ADHD friends who asked to use it too. That's how I know it's working. If it solves my problem authentically, it solves theirs. Built for me, useful for us.
