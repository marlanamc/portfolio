October 26, 2025

Continuous verbs in context

Thinking about how I can teach continuous verbs more efficiently. My students can form them perfectly but in context they get very confused.

The problem isn't conjugation, it's knowing when to use the continuous form versus simple present. They'll say "I watch TV" when they mean "I'm watching TV right now" because they don't recognize the time indicators.

Thinking about a game activity that really focuses on time indicators and expressions. Words like "now," "at the moment," "this week," "currently." If students can identify those cues, they'll know to use continuous forms. Visual timeline with scenarios might work better than drills.

Comparative adjectives are harder than they look

Students in my ESOL class are struggling with comparative adjectives. Not just "big/bigger" but the nuanced cases like "more interesting" vs "more boring" and when to use more vs the -er suffix.

Flashcards don't work well here because comparatives are relational. You need context (compared to what?) to use them correctly. Students aren't just memorizing vocabulary; they're learning a grammatical pattern with exceptions.

Teaching sentence structures like "more/less than...", "whereas", and "both ... and" were really challenging for them. Going back and reviewing comparatives extensively seemed to help. Reviewing connectors like because, so, but, and while helped tie these together.

Thinking about building a drag-and-drop game where students compare two things using visual prompts. Instead of flashcards with "fast" → "faster", show a race car vs a bicycle and ask: "The car is _____ than the bike." The comparison creates meaning rather than just recalling a word.

Could add audio feedback. Students hear "The elephant is louder than the mouse" with sound, reinforcing the concept multiple ways. Audio + visuals + interactive construction works better than flashcards.

Why I rewrote my ESOL games three times

Built these games because flashcards weren't working for my students. First version was for my Blogger site, simple and functional but basic. Second version this summer: cleaned up the code and made them more interactive. Third version now: added challenge and practice modes, better feedback loops.

Each rewrite taught me something: first version taught me user needs over perfect code. Second version taught me the importance of maintainable structure. Third version taught me that small UX tweaks, like separate modes, can make tools way more useful.

Practice mode lets students see the correct answer immediately. Challenge mode times them and tracks streaks. Same content, different learning styles. Found that some students prefer one mode over the other, which is why offering both mattered.

Building for myself first

Built the ADHD First Aid Kit for myself when I'm stuck and overwhelmed. Every productivity app assumes I can "just focus" or "break tasks into smaller steps." Those don't work when your brain doesn't do linear thinking.

The whole thing started because I kept hitting walls. Not "can't decide what to do next" walls, but "brain literally won't process information" walls. So I made a toolkit of things that actually help: timers, focus tools, sensory-friendly interfaces. All in one place.

When I showed this to other neurodivergent friends, they asked if they could use it. That's how I know I'm building something useful. If it solves my problem authentically, it solves theirs too.
